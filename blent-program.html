You are not unfit. On your first attempt you missed the pass by two seconds — then you got slower, training flat out seven days a week. So this version does the opposite of piling on: we take the impact off, clear the fatigue, keep one sharp run a week, and build a buffer big enough that a nervous day still passes. What I changed, and why

1
Saturday run — cut. You have three hours of netball that day. A run on top is just extra pounding for no gain.
2
Monday & Thursday runs — mostly non-impact now. Bike, row or cross-trainer builds the same aerobic engine without the joint load. Only your one weekly quality session stays on legs.

Time trials moved to Friday. Your lightest day. I want your benchmark run fresh, not dragged down by the day before — that’s how we get honest numbers.
4
Recovery is now part of the plan, not an afterthought. See the traffic-light guide below — if you’re wrecked, you back off. That’s the program working, not you failing.

One for your DS and me to talk through. Your external load — three netball sessions, gym, skiing — is the real fatigue driver. Non-impact cardio — how to run it

Any of these work. Pick what’s available and what your body likes. The point is aerobic work with the joints protected — the same engine, none of the pounding.

Stationary bike
Easiest to control effort. Great for both steady and intervals.
Rower
Full-body aerobic. Keep the stroke smooth, drive with the legs.
Cross-trainer
Closest to a running feel with zero impact.
Ski-erg
Strong aerobic hit, easy on the legs after netball.
Aqua jog
Deep-water running — the best run-transfer of the lot, truly zero impact.
Swim
Total offload. Good on your most beaten-up days.
Zone 2 · easyConversational, RPE 4–5. You could talk in full sentences. This is most of your non-impact work.
ThresholdComfortably hard, RPE 6–7. A sentence, not a paragraph. Used in the non-impact "tempo/interval" sessions.
IntervalsHard efforts with easy recovery. Every rep the same effort — controlled, not a sprint.
07

Reading your fatigue — the traffic light

Check in with yourself before each of my sessions. How you feel decides how you train that day. This is a skill, not a weakness — the best athletes auto-regulate.

Green — good to go

Slept well, legs feel fine, keen. Run the session as written, including the optional Friday.

Amber — a bit flat

Tired but functional. Do the session, drop the last rep or two, skip anything optional. Don’t chase times.

Red — wrecked

Sore, drained, poor sleep, or netball has hammered you. Swap for easy non-impact or rest. Tell me — backing off here protects the retest.

Sleep is the multiplier. 7–9 hours does more for a fatigued athlete than any extra session. If something has to give on a big week, make it a workout — never the sleep.

08

Fuelling — to run faster

Read this first: the goal is to fuel to perform and recover, not to diet. Training this much on too little food makes you slower and keeps you fatigued — the opposite of what we want. A leaner, more efficient runner is the by-product of good training plus eating well, never of restriction. This is principles, not calorie targets.

The plate — most meals

Half the plate
Veg & salad
Volume, fibre, micronutrients. Fills you up without weighing you down.
A quarter
Lean protein
Palm-sized. Eggs, chicken, fish, yoghurt, legumes, tofu. Every meal — it drives recovery, which is the whole game right now.
A quarter
Quality carbs
Rice, oats, potato, wholegrain, fruit. Your fuel — with this much training, under-eating carbs is why legs feel dead.
Before training

A light carb source 1–2 hrs out — banana, toast, oats. Never hit the quality run or netball on empty.

After training

Protein + carbs within the hour — yoghurt & fruit, eggs on toast, a proper meal. This is where you actually recover.

Hydration

Steady water across the day. Even mild dehydration slows the run and deepens fatigue. Pale straw, not dark.

On heavy days

Netball + skiing days burn a lot — eat more, not less, or you’ll pay for it with a flat week.

Stay in lane. This is general fuelling to support training — not a prescribed diet. If you want specific body-composition targets or an intake plan, that’s a dietitian’s call and Defence Health can refer. If food ever starts feeling stressful, tell me — that’s a health conversation, not a harder push.

09

The head game

That 31-second regression is where the nerves — and the fatigue — showed up. Fix the head and the recovery, and part of the gap closes on its own.

Race the process, not the outcome

You can’t control "pass." You can control hitting 2:28 on lap one. Run your splits, and the result follows — the pressure lifts off the finish line.

The buffer is the safety net

Every session at 14:30 pace is proof banked. On the day you’re not chasing a number you’ve never hit — you’re repeating something you’ve done all block, with 30 seconds to spare.

Reframe the record

You’re not "someone who failed." You’re someone who missed by two seconds while overtrained — and is now doing it properly. Same facts, a completely different story to walk to the line with.

Pre-run routine — same every time

1Identical warm-up you’ve rehearsed all block — the body recognises it and settles.
2Four rounds of box breathing (in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4) to bring the nerves down.
3One cue word for lap one: "controlled." Say it, run to it.
4Lock onto lap splits, not the total. One lap at a time, breathing to cadence.
5From lap 5, one permission: spend everything that’s left.

Outside PT scope. If the anxiety runs deeper than test nerves, this sits alongside — not instead of — Psych or Chaplain support. Worth making sure you’re linked in. I’ve got the running; that side has people who are better placed than me.
